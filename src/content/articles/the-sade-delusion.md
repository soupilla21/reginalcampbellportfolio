*Or: Why I'm Hearing Sade and My Wife Is Hearing Big Sean*

Every man in a long marriage has had this moment. You're in the car. Maybe dinner's on the stove. Somebody puts on Sade, and the opening of "Is It a Crime" comes through the speakers.

And your brain goes to work.

You look over at your wife. In your head, she's not just listening to the song. She's singing it. To you. Every word about wanting somebody more than she's ever wanted anything is aimed straight at your chest. You are the reason this woman has feelings this big.

You sit up a little straighter. You might adjust your collar.

I know how this looks. A grown man, posing for a music video nobody is filming, in a Dodge, at a red light. I know. I do it anyway. Every time.

I call this the Sade Delusion. I've had it for thirty years.

## The research

I have been studying this question for three decades. One research subject. Same subject the whole time. My wife.

She is a real Sade fan. She knows the catalog. She respects the voice. She has heard "Is It a Crime" more times than either of us can count, on road trips, over dinners, in kitchens and restaurants.

Number of times I have caught her singing it about me: zero.

## What's actually playing in her head

Here's what I've come to believe. While I'm sitting there casting myself as the man Sade is crying over, my wife has a completely different song running.

Big Sean. "I Don't Fuck With You."

Yes. That one.

If you missed it: it's a breakup record where Big Sean spends the whole song listing everything he would rather be doing than dealing with his ex, and celebrating the fact that he got away clean. It became the anthem for anybody who ever looked at somebody and thought, "I'm good. Thank you."

Now hold both of those in your head at the same time.

On the outside: Sade. Velvet. Saxophone. A woman so in love it hurts.

On the inside: a profane, bass-heavy breakup record, playing at full volume behind the eyes of a woman calmly eating trail mix across the table from me. Not angry. Not upset. Just vibing. Nodding along to a song about how glad she is to be rid of somebody, while the somebody sits right there, smiling, thinking he's the reason for the saxophone.

That's the energy. Not heartbreak. Not longing. Relief, set to a beat.

## Fantasy versus reality

**What I picture:** I walk into the living room. Lights low. Sade on. My wife on the couch, one tear rolling down her face. She looks up at me like I'm the last man on Earth and she can't believe her luck.

**What actually happens:** I walk into the living room. Sade on. My wife on the couch, scrolling Amazon for another thing we do not need. In her head, she is not overwhelmed by my presence. She is quietly running through Big Sean's list of things she'd rather be doing.

She's too polite to say it out loud.

Most of the time.

## The anniversary

The illusion officially died on our thirtieth anniversary.

Nice restaurant. Both of us feeling sentimental. A live band started a slow jazz version of "Is It a Crime," and I thought, this is it. Thirty years. This is the moment she finally tells me.

I reached across the table. Took her hand. Gave her my best look.

The singer hit the chorus.

My wife squeezed my hand, leaned in close, and said, "I think I'm getting the chocolate lava cake. It'd be a crime not to."

She got the lava cake. It was excellent. My moment was gone.

## The verdict

Here's what I figured out, and it only took me thirty-something years.

I need a soundtrack. She needs a plan. I'm out here waiting for my cinematic moment, and she's running the house: the bills, the calendar, the laundry, and a fourth set of storage bins that Amazon swears will change our lives. I'm the guy waiting for the slow-motion shot. She's the one who made sure we got to the restaurant on time.

My wife's love was never a Sade song. There's no drama in it. It's in the tea she remembers to buy. It's in the eye roll she gives me when I tell the same joke for the hundredth time, and the little smile she can't hold back right after. It's thirty years of inside jokes and quiet Sunday mornings.

That kind of love doesn't have to ask whether it's a crime. It's settled. It knows exactly who I am.

I'm not the man a Sade song gets written about.

I'm the man a Sade song plays in front of while she decides what's for dinner.
