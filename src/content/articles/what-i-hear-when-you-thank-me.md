*Five words, said with good intentions. This is about what they leave out.*

Every Veterans Day, somebody thanks me for my service.

A coworker. A cashier who sees the hat. A LinkedIn notification from someone I haven't talked to in years. The words are always the same, and so is my answer. I nod. I say thank you back. And then the moment is over, and we both move on.

I know how to answer it. I've never known how to feel about it. The people saying it mean well, and most of them mean it sincerely. But the phrase has never landed for me. It feels like a receipt handed across a counter. Transaction complete. Conversation over.

I've been trying to figure out why it bothers me without turning into the guy who's angry at people for being polite. Gratitude and honesty can sit in the same chair. This is my attempt at both.

"Thank you for your service" is a complete sentence. Too often, it becomes the whole conversation.

## Twenty-one years old

I left for the Army at 20. I arrived at my first duty station a freshly minted 21-year-old, on my way to getting married.

I went in as a kid who answered to "Soup." I came out a Staff Sergeant, responsible for other people's sons and daughters.

Very early in my enlistment, we lost a soldier in a vehicle accident.

There was no mission. No enemy. Just a return from some much-needed time off, the kind of break everybody looks forward to. And then a soldier was gone.

What struck me then, and what stays with me now, is how hard we all took it and how fast we went back to work.

Say your goodbyes. Cry. Salute. Keep it moving to the next order of business.

That isn't coldness. It's the job. The mission doesn't pause for grief, so you learn to carry grief while the mission keeps going. I learned that before I was old enough to understand what I was learning.

That's what I hear inside "thank you for your service." Not the flag. That.

## Not the television version

When most people picture what a service member risks, they picture the raid at night or the convoy under fire. Those things are real. But a lot of the people we lose are never lost in combat. They're lost in training, in accidents, in the ordinary days of a life where the risk never fully switches off.

I'm not saying that to make us sound special. I'm saying it because military life costs people in ways that never make the news.

The Army teaches you to keep functioning while carrying things the rest of the world never sees. Then someone says five words, and you're supposed to find an answer that accounts for all of it.

## Why I won't just sell it

I know what it's like to be on the receiving end of a thank-you that skips the hard parts. So when someone asks me about joining, I don't skip them.

Every so often, a parent asks me to talk to their son or daughter about enlisting. They want me to convince them.

I can't do that blindly.

I'll tell them what the Army gave me. Discipline. Purpose. A sense that the team matters more than any one person on it. That lesson has shaped how I've led every team since.

But I also tell them the truth. Whatever brings you to the recruiting office, whether it's school, a paycheck, a way out, or a sense of duty, understand what you're agreeing to. You're committing to serve your country. And that commitment can cost you your life before you ever see a battlefield.

I want them to sit with that before they decide. If I leave it out, I'm not doing them a favor.

## Two uniforms, one marriage

My wife was in the Army too.

That's the part people don't expect, and it's the part that explains how we made it. Because throughout our enlistments, I was gone. I mean always gone. Some years it felt like I was gone more than I was home.

In my Army career, doing well often meant being gone more. Once I decided I wanted to excel, the training exercises and leadership courses multiplied. Twenty-one days here. Three months there. Every step up came with another set of orders taking me somewhere that wasn't home.

Every soldier knows Jody, the guy in the marching cadences who's home while you're gone. I joked about him like everybody else. But the joke was never really about him. It was about how often I wasn't there.

My wife carried that. She had her own mission and her own chain of command, and she was also holding things together while I was somewhere else. Two full commitments at once, and she kept both.

Missed birthdays became normal. Then missed anniversaries. Then every special occasion. After a while we stopped measuring our marriage by the dates on the calendar. We learned to enjoy whatever time we had together, on any regular day we got.

It wasn't how we expected our marriage to work. It was how we learned to make it work.

So if you thank me for my service, thank her too. She did it twice: once in her own uniform, and once waiting on me.

## About the benefits

Here is the part I've been careful with, because it's where I get angry.

Every so often I hear someone grumble about what veterans get. The education benefit. The hiring preference. The idea that we're cutting the line.

I understand how it looks from outside. From outside it looks like a perk.

From inside it looks like a trade. Those benefits weren't handed out. They were promised in exchange for years that didn't belong to us. Years when we went where we were sent, missed what we missed, and accepted a level of risk most jobs never ask of anyone, including on days when nobody was shooting. The benefit is the back half of a deal. The front half was already paid, and some people paid it in full.

Most people have never had a reason to see that front half, and I don't hold that against them. What I can't accept is enjoying what that service protected while treating the people who provided it as if they're getting away with something.

I'm not asking anyone to feel guilty. I'm asking them to look at the whole trade before they judge half of it.

## What I'd ask for instead

So here's what I'd ask, today and every day after.

**If you want to thank a veteran, make room for a conversation.** Ask where they served. Ask what they miss. Then stay for the answer, however short it is. And if they'd rather not talk about it, accept that just as gracefully. The point isn't to collect a story. It's to leave the door open longer than five words.

**If you lead veterans, make room for the experience they bring.** Don't assume you know what it was, and don't ask them to explain more than they want to. The person on your team who stays calm when everything breaks learned that somewhere. You don't need the story to respect that it exists.

I'll still nod when someone thanks me today. I'll still say thank you back. But behind the nod, I'll be carrying all of it. A soldier who never made it back from time off. Birthdays my wife and I celebrated on whatever day we happened to be in the same place. Years that belonged to the Army before they belonged to us.

Five words can't hold that. They were never meant to. But they can open a door, if you're willing to stay for one more question.
