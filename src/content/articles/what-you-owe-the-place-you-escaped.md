*Success can change your ZIP code, your options, your network, and what becomes possible for your children. It doesn't erase what you owe the people who never got those same choices.*

I spent my formative years in K-Town, a section of North Lawndale on Chicago's West Side. It was a place full of intelligence, humor, and ambition, of families and friendships, of people working hard to build decent lives. They were doing it inside circumstances that routinely made that harder than it needed to be. The neighborhood was poor, and resources were limited. Drugs and crime were not abstractions or things you read about somewhere else; they were part of the environment.

I was never physically trapped there. For elementary school, I traveled to the North Side. Later, I attended Whitney Young, one of the city's high-performing magnet high schools. My mother did everything she could to expose me to more of the city, and busing myself to school every day gave me the knowledge and the nerve to go almost anywhere and be comfortable once I got there. I was moving between versions of Chicago: leaving one environment in the morning, spending the day around different resources and expectations, and returning home at night. Along the way I developed an instinct for when to shrink in a room and when I could stand tall.

That exposure mattered, not because I discovered that smart people existed outside my neighborhood, but because I discovered that the conditions I knew were not inevitable. Poverty was not normal. There was nothing natural about it.

But exposure is not the same thing as access. I wasn't trapped. I was resource-constrained. I could see the people and places on the other side of the city, but I didn't have the money to actually take part in them. So I found ways to get money for the basics and the small wants I could get my hands on, sometimes through choices born more of constraint than ambition. The details matter less than the lesson: I learned early the difference between being able to see a door and being able to walk through it.

The Army widened the aperture again, and it taught me something I now think sits at the center of this whole question. The Army cared far less about where I came from than whether I could meet the standard, and it built the path to get there: training, clear expectations, defined criteria for advancement, and leaders whose job was to develop the people under them. It is not a perfect institution. But it was proof that when a system is deliberately designed to move people up, it does. Talent was never what K-Town was missing. Design was.

Those lessons have stayed with me. So has a question I think about more as I get older: What do you owe the place you escaped?

## The Illusion of "Making It Out"

I struggle with the word "escaped," even as I use it.

There is something about saying someone "made it out" that has always bothered me. Out of what? Into what? We use the phrase as if crossing an income threshold, changing neighborhoods, earning degrees, or building a career moves a person onto another planet. There are the people who made it out, and there are the people who didn't.

That framing quietly becomes a judgment. I figured it out. Why didn't you? I made better choices. Why didn't you?

Maybe some people did make better choices. Maybe some worked harder. Personal agency matters, and acknowledging structural barriers doesn't require pretending individual decisions are meaningless. But I know too many intelligent people to believe our outcomes can be explained that neatly. I am not an extraordinary exception to a community that lacked ability. I was exposed to things. I encountered opportunities. Doors opened, and I walked through them.

I can be proud of that story without turning it into evidence that everyone who stayed simply failed to do the same. Sometimes success is proof of perseverance. Sometimes it is also proof that access matters. Both can be true.

## What Access Requires

When people talk about the burden of success, they usually mean the personal strain: the exhaustion of helping, the requests that never stop, the tension of managing everyone's expectations. For me, the burden has never been the requests themselves. Being open and available to others hasn't cost me anything of note, and my wife has been wonderfully supportive of the ways I try to give back.

The weight I carry comes from knowing I could do more.

It also comes from knowing exactly what a door is worth. I once had to decide whether to fire someone who looked like me, close to my age, highly skilled and intelligent. I knew how hard it is for someone from where we came from to reach that level. I went through the options again and again, knowing a door like that might never open for them again. In the end, I let them go. Responsibility didn't mean avoiding the decision. It meant understanding the cost before I made it.

When I can open doors, I try to do it deliberately. I've bought rental property in neighborhoods with strong public schools and rented units to family and friends below market rate. That means their children attend those schools while their families pay less than the market would charge. I'm not pretending it's charity; the properties are still investments. But access to a strong school shouldn't be reserved only for people who can pay full market price.

None of that makes me a hero. It makes me someone who understands what my position can open, and what it can close.

I hold myself to this because I've seen the alternative. There are people in my position, and far beyond it, who speak eloquently about the conditions in neighborhoods like mine and do nothing to change them. Concern without action is commentary. I don't want to be a commentator.

If you have acquired knowledge, connections, money, credibility, influence, or access that someone else does not have, you have the ability to change another person's trajectory. Once you know that, neutrality is a choice, and usually a comfortable one.

## The Goal Isn't to Climb Back Down

Sometimes we talk about staying connected to disadvantaged communities as though solidarity requires proximity to struggle. It doesn't.

Nobody needs to move back into the neighborhood they left. Nobody needs to send their child to an under-resourced school to prove they care. The goal cannot be to make successful people recreate the conditions they worked to leave.

My children don't owe K-Town anything. They get the stability, safety, and networks my wife and I worked to build, and I will never weaponize my past to burden their future. They are not repayment plans for my journey. What I hope they inherit isn't guilt. It's the understanding that comfort is not an excuse for indifference.

The point isn't to climb back down the ladder. It's to stop pulling it up behind you.

You keep climbing. You get into rooms nobody from your block used to be invited into. You learn things nobody explained to you. You accumulate influence as a hiring manager, an executive, an investor, a mentor, or the person whose phone call actually gets returned. You are no longer only navigating the gate. In some rooms, you are part of it.

And then you use some of it. Not recklessly. Deliberately.

Holding the door open can mean referring someone before a job is ever posted. It can mean paying the certification fee that keeps someone out. It can mean explaining the unwritten rules of a room to someone walking into it for the first time. It can mean offering a paid internship instead of "exposure." It can mean giving someone a clearer map, or a real introduction instead of a vague offer to help. None of it is heroic. All of it is a choice.

## What Remains

When I look back at K-Town now, knowing what I know and holding the keys I hold today, my reaction isn't nostalgia, and it isn't pity. It's blunter than that:

Damn. How the fuck is anybody supposed to build a stable life coming from this place?

When you truly internalize what it takes just to survive an environment where so many things are working against you, one thing becomes clear: merit is real, but it is never the whole explanation.

I don't feel guilty that my life became different. I'm grateful. And gratitude, taken seriously, asks something of us. Not that we save everyone. Not that we sacrifice our children or pretend poverty is a virtue.

Just that when we find ourselves standing beside a door somebody once helped us through, we remember there is someone behind us.

And we hold it open.
