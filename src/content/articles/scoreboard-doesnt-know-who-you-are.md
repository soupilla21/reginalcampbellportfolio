*A scoreboard measures an outcome, and too many leaders let it measure people.*

There was a stretch of my college basketball career when success meant losing by fewer than 20 points. Lose by 12? Respectable. Lose by eight? Hell, we might have been onto something. Win? Now you're just being unreasonable.

I had already served in the Army. Now I was playing basketball at the Illinois Institute of Technology, trying to square everything I believed about preparation, discipline, and execution with another 30-point loss. The scoreboard wasn't wrong. I was asking it to tell me more than it could.

## Recruited in a Hallway

Growing up in Chicago, I had hoop dreams. Like a lot of kids, I imagined what basketball might become for me, and the possibilities were considerably more impressive than my actual abilities. Basketball eventually settled that argument. I wasn't going to be the next great Chicago basketball player. I wasn't going to be the next pretty good one either.

I joined the Army, served my country, and came back to civilian life with plans that had very little to do with basketball. I enrolled in junior college, and one day I was walking down a hallway when the basketball coach stopped me and asked me to join the team. They were short on players. I was tall. That's not the recruiting story you see in a documentary.

He also figured my military background, and the fact that I was older than everyone else on the roster, might bring something the team was missing: leadership, maturity, experience. And height. I don't know how he ranked those qualities, but I suspect height did most of the work.

I played that season, then decided basketball was done. I was older than my teammates, and I had a plan: get into the Illinois Institute of Technology and get on with the next chapter. Basketball had been a nice detour. Or so I thought.

After I enrolled at IIT, my junior college coach called out of the blue and told me to go see the basketball coach at Illinois Tech. I didn't expect much. That coach offered me a scholarship to play college basketball. Me, the guy whose recruiting credentials amounted to being tall, older, and conveniently located in a junior college hallway.

I was elated. There was no recruiting process, no highlight film, no programs calling. One coach noticed me in a hallway, another made a phone call, and a door I thought had closed opened again. It wasn't the version of the dream I'd imagined as a kid, but it was mine.

Then we started playing games.

## When Preparation Doesn't Move the Score

The Army taught me to approach a mission with preparation, discipline, accountability, and the expectation that the team would execute. You didn't casually shrug off failure. At Illinois Tech, we could prepare all week, practice hard, show up ready to compete, and still lose by 30. Thirty isn't a game decided by a questionable call or a lucky shot. It's an entire evening of somebody else having a much better time than you.

And it kept happening. We tried different things. We worked, we competed, and we wanted to win. The results weren't interested in our effort. Coming out of the military, losing like that felt like failing a mission, except there was no obvious adjustment that fixed it. We'd go back, prepare, try again, and land on the wrong side of another ugly score. I was frustrated beyond belief. I'd dreamed of playing college basketball, and some nights it felt like I'd signed up for a recurring nightmare with sneakers.

What made it worse was that I cared about the game, about competing, and about the guys next to me, who were putting real effort into something that rarely paid them back. Eventually, you run out of explanations. You can't blame every loss on the officials. You can't claim the other team got lucky when they beat you by 25. And you can't keep talking about moral victories when the actual victories are getting harder to find. Sometimes the other team is simply better. Sometimes it's a whole lot better. That's hard to accept when you pride yourself on preparation, effort, and execution, and none of the three is changing the outcome.

I wasn't the best player on that team. Not close. But I found a role. My teammates valued my effort, my willingness to compete, and my ability to express dissatisfaction in language that wasn't suitable for family audiences. My military vocabulary transferred to basketball far better than my shooting did. I could cuss, and I did.

I also cared about those guys and wanted them to succeed. If that meant pushing someone in practice, encouraging him during a game, or bringing energy when we had every reason to be discouraged, I did it. I wasn't going to carry the team with my scoring; that much had been established. But I could contribute, and in return, my teammates made me feel like I belonged.

Still, the losses muted almost everything else. It's hard to notice someone's improvement while you're getting blown out, and hard to celebrate progress in practice when the next game ends the same way. The work was real. The commitment was real. So were the losses. When winning becomes the only measure that matters, everything else gets harder to see.

## What the Scoreboard Can't Tell You

The scoreboard never lied. When we lost by 30, we lost by 30. There was no column for effort, determination, or how badly we wanted it, and nobody added points because we were good teammates. The scoreboard told us the result. It couldn't tell us everything the game meant, or everything about the people who played it.

It didn't measure what a scholarship meant to a guy who had already accepted that his playing days were over. It didn't measure the relationships, the improvement, or what it took to keep showing up when the results gave us almost nothing back. And it was never qualified to say what kind of people we were.

That's where the trouble starts. We take an instrument built to measure an outcome and use it to measure identity. We do it in sports, and we're even better at it at work.

Take a promotion. Two people compete for a leadership role, and one gets it. Almost immediately, the story writes itself. The selected candidate has strong leadership, executive presence, and strategic thinking, and the organization made the right call. The person who wasn't selected gets feedback about development opportunities, readiness, or some quality that apparently wasn't quite sufficient.

Maybe one candidate really did demonstrate stronger capabilities. The decision may have been sound. But being the better choice for a particular role isn't the same as being the better leader in every setting, and being passed over doesn't make someone incapable of leading.

Too often, we start with the outcome and work backward, assigning the winner every quality we admire and the loser every deficiency we need to explain the result. Winners were disciplined, talented, and deserving. Losers weren't prepared, didn't want it badly enough, or couldn't perform when it mattered. Sometimes that's true. Often, we don't have enough information to know. Acknowledging who won isn't the problem. Pretending the result tells you everything worth knowing about the people involved is.

## Accountability Without a Verdict

A loss gives you something to assess about your performance. It doesn't give you a verdict on your worth.

We weren't a good basketball team, and I'm not going to rewrite that. We had weaknesses, other teams exploited them, and we lost games we badly wanted to win. Nobody needed to tell us we were secretly champions. We needed to get better. That's accountability. But between "we weren't good enough to win" and "here's what that says about us as people," there's a line that doesn't need to be crossed.

I didn't always see that line. The military reinforced owning outcomes, and I still believe in preparation, discipline, and execution. But basketball exposed an assumption I hadn't examined: you can be fully responsible for your preparation and performance and still not control the result. That's not an excuse. It's how competition works, and leaders who evaluate other people need to understand it.

A leader owes the team an honest assessment of what happened. If the plan failed, say so. If execution was poor, address it. If someone didn't meet the standard, don't pretend they did. But telling someone their work fell short is different from treating them like they are the shortcoming. One gives them something to improve. The other gives them something to carry.

At work, that distinction can disappear quickly. A missed target becomes a judgment about commitment. An unsuccessful project becomes evidence of weak leadership. A rejected idea becomes proof that the person who proposed it lacks judgment. Sometimes the criticism is earned. Good leadership still requires the discipline to separate what happened, why it happened, and what it actually tells you about the people responsible.

Accountability and self-worth aren't enemies. You can own a shortcoming without letting it become your identity, and you can hold someone to a high standard without reducing them to the last time they missed it.

## Losing Teaches Nothing on Its Own

We love saying failure is the best teacher. It looks good on a motivational poster, and it sounds terrific coming from somebody who just accepted a lifetime achievement award. But failure doesn't automatically teach you anything.

Some people lose repeatedly and never change a damn thing. They blame the circumstances, question the process, criticize the competition, and decide the outcome had nothing to do with their performance. Then they show up to the next competition with the same preparation, the same habits, and the same blind spots, and act surprised when the result looks familiar. That's not resilience. That's repetition.

Losing becomes useful only when you examine what happened and do something with what you learn. Maybe you weren't prepared. Maybe the strategy was flawed. Maybe the effort wasn't enough. Or maybe someone else was simply better.

That last possibility is the hardest one for people who pride themselves on being competitive. I've met plenty of people who describe themselves as great competitors. They hate losing, they demand excellence, and winning is in their DNA. Fine. But hating to lose isn't impressive; almost nobody enjoys it. What matters is what happens afterward. Can you acknowledge the outcome without rewriting history? Recognize someone else's excellence without treating it as an insult to your own? Admit you weren't good enough that day without deciding you never will be?

A real competitor understands that losing is part of the arrangement. You don't get to demand the opportunity to compete while insisting the outcome validate your opinion of yourself. That's not competition. That's a request for applause with a scoreboard attached.

At Illinois Tech, I wanted to win every game, and some of those losses made me angry. Anger didn't make me a better player. Neither did wishing the other team weren't so good. I still had to face what I could do, what I couldn't do, and what the team needed from me.

Sometimes that meant working on a weakness. Mine was boxing out. Not because I was thin, but because I'd been used to being the tallest person on the court. Now I was battling players six inches taller than me, and the height that got me recruited in a hallway wasn't going to bail me out. I had to learn to establish position and anticipate where the ball would go.

Sometimes it meant accepting a role that was never going to show up in the box score. I became the guy who inbounded the ball. I hated it at first. Then I learned to relish it. My coach trusted my decision-making and precision, and I began to value the job differently. It didn't matter how high somebody could jump or how good the play looked on paper. If I couldn't get the ball inbounds, none of it mattered.

And sometimes it meant admitting we had worked hard and still weren't good enough to beat the team across from us. That isn't surrender. It's being honest enough about reality to decide what comes next.

## What I Actually Won

More than 20 years have passed since those basketball days. I don't remember how many games we lost, which is probably for the best. I couldn't tell you most of the final scores, and I certainly couldn't give you a compelling statistical breakdown of my college career. Trust me, nobody is missing out.

But I remember the guys: the locker room, the conversations, the stories, and the kind of humor that develops when a group of people spends enough time working toward something that refuses to cooperate. More than two decades later, I'm still connected to some of those teammates. I've watched their lives unfold and their children grow up. We've stayed part of one another's stories long after the final buzzer.

At the time, I couldn't fully see that. I was too busy wanting to win, and I don't regret it. We were there to compete, and losing by 30 wasn't a character development program I'd signed up for. But winning wasn't the only thing happening. We were building relationships that would outlast our playing days. I still wish we'd won more games. I wouldn't trade those relationships to erase the losses.

We lost a lot of basketball games. The scoreboard was right about every one of them.

It just never knew who we were.
