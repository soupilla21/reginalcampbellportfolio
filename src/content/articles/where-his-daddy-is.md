*What my younger cousin remembered about the walks she couldn’t join.*

My younger cousin sent me a voice message recently that included a fairly detailed description of a suspect.

“I remember you would come over. Like once a week or once every other week. And you would have that UPS shirt on, with your locks, and was tall.”

Me. The suspect was me. She remembered the visits, the shirt, the hair. But none of those details explained her real problem with my showing up.

“My dad was like, ‘Okay, we about to go for a walk. We’ll be back.’ And I would beg and beg and beg and say, ‘Can I go?’ And he would tell me no, I couldn’t go. Cuz y’all have stuff to talk about.”

Her father and I would leave. She would stay behind, thoroughly dissatisfied with the arrangement.

“And when y’all would leave, I’d be so pissed off. And I’m like, this some bullshit, where his daddy is, why he taking my dad?”

There it was. A complaint, delivered years after the offense. She remembered being a little girl who wanted her father, and she remembered me as the person who kept taking him away.

She was right about what she saw. We kept leaving without her. That didn’t make the walks wrong. It meant I had never thought much about the part of them she lived.

My father had died. My uncle, her father, had become a stand-in for mine, a sounding board, someone I could talk things through with. We took long, meandering walks through the neighborhood, and more often than not they ended at Fosco Park.

My uncle fancied himself my favorite uncle back then. He was after the crown. Anyone who knew my actual favorite uncle could have told him that title was never changing hands. But there was always something special about those walks. They were a scheduled, informal way for us to bond.

We talked about everything. No single lesson stands out. What stands out is the conviction he stood on. He believed fervently in God, and just as fervently that I was destined for greatness. He met my cynical, dismissive talk about faith and religion with that same conviction. His instruction and counsel fortified my view of generosity and servant leadership.

She wanted her daddy. I missed mine.

From her perspective, a grown man kept showing up to borrow someone she wanted to herself. Then her message took a turn: “And then, I got older, and I realized what y’all was going to go talk about.”

I won’t try to explain what she came to understand. What caught me was how much detail she kept. I knew those walks had stayed with me. I hadn’t realized how much of them had stayed with her.

Her message made room for a third person in a memory I had treated as belonging to two.

At work, it is easy to mistake the people involved in a decision for everyone whose experience needs to be understood. We record who attended the meeting, who approved the plan, who owns the work. We can produce a very respectable account of an event without including anyone who wasn’t invited.

Then the people outside the room have to make sense of what happened inside it.

At UPS, the people who sorted and delivered the packages had their own accounts of my decisions about the sort, usually in more colorful language than anything said in the planning meeting. I knew what I had intended. They knew what it did to their work.

That does not mean she should have joined every walk. Private conversations are allowed to remain private. Nor does every decision need every voice in the room. But responsibility does not end with the people included in the conversation. Their distance from the decision does not make their experience irrelevant.

The uncomfortable part is that we may have to hear their account before we recognize what ours leaves out.

A leader can ask for candor and then spend the response explaining why the other person has misunderstood. By the end, the intentions are fully defended and the original experience has disappeared. I can see the temptation. I had a compelling reason for those visits. My father was gone. I needed that relationship. Yet my reason does not cancel a little girl’s wish to have her father stay.

Holding both takes more care than defending either one. It asks me to listen without treating another person’s account as a threat to my own.

When her message came in, I laughed. It was funny, and it was heartwarming. It also got us talking about other small moments from back then.

There is a small twist in this family saga. The walks that took her father away seem to have brought the two of us closer. These days I am, by my own account, her favorite cousin. Then again, her father once believed he was my favorite uncle. Somewhere out there, she may have a secret favorite cousin. I wonder who it is.

Her father and I still talk about those walks. They mattered to us.

Now I know she remembers them, too. Only her memory starts with us leaving.
