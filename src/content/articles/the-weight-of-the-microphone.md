*Facts should survive verification. Convictions should survive being identified as convictions.*

I don't think most leaders who mislead the people listening to them ever set out to. The failure is quieter than that: speaking with the confidence of fact when what they actually hold is a conviction.

A platform doesn't require a stage. It can be a boardroom, a classroom, a pulpit, a podcast, a LinkedIn following, or a team that looks at you before making a hard call. Sometimes it's just being the person in the room whose opinion people trust. And influence makes certainty feel good.

What we know, what we believe, and what we don't know yet are three different things. Under the pressure of an audience, they can start to sound identical. If I'm citing research, I should be able to tell you where it came from. If I'm describing history, I should care whether I've got it right. If I'm offering a conviction, I should be willing to call it that. "This is what I know" and "this is what I believe" are both legitimate statements, but they are not the same statement.

The more people are listening, the more it matters which one you're making. When you're given the privilege of speaking to people and in front of them, you're held to a higher standard: be factual in your words, and say so plainly when what you're offering is your belief. That isn't a hedge on conviction. It's respect for the person listening, who deserves to know what they're being handed.

The microphone adds weight.

## Where I learned to hear it

I grew up in a church-going, Holy Ghost-catching, all-day-sermon family. My grandfather and my uncle were both well-known bishops, so the comings and goings of church folks were never foreign to me. Over the years I saw a lot of leaders up close, in a lot of pulpits and a lot of rooms.

Some of what I saw was admirable: people who understood that the ones listening weren't just hearing words. They were extending trust, and those leaders handled that trust carefully.

I also watched the opposite. I watched personal preference become principle, interpretation become fact, and confidence become evidence. And I watched people make very real decisions based on something a trusted voice declared, never realizing they were hearing one person's conviction rather than settled truth.

What made that damaging wasn't that anyone was wrong. Everyone is wrong sometimes. The damage was invisible: nobody listening could tell conviction from established fact, so nobody knew when to question it, verify it, or make the decision for themselves.

Recently, a longtime friend and I had an animated, powerful conversation about this. We were talking about something church leaders do so often it can stop registering: speaking as if they know exactly what God or Jesus is thinking. Not "I believe." Not "this is my understanding." A declaration, delivered with the confidence of a direct report.

I'll concede what needs conceding. Faith lives partly in language, and people have always taken some literary freedom with religion, often under the cover of "we know your heart." But faith is belief by its nature. Presenting it as a statement of fact disguises what kind of claim it is. The listener can still decide, but they're deciding without knowing what they've been handed.

Where my friend and I landed is that this isn't a church problem. It's a microphone problem. I've spent my career since then in other hierarchies, and I've watched the same dynamic play out in every one of them.

The lesson I took from it is simple. When people trust you, they stop checking your work. That doesn't lower the bar for what you say. It means you're the only one left holding it.

## The honest leader pays a price

Here's the part the easy version of this argument skips: audiences reward certainty. The leader who says, "Here's the part I'm less sure about," can lose the room to the one who never admits doubt. Confidence reads as competence in interviews, board meetings, classrooms, and social feeds alike. If intellectual honesty costs influence in the short run, the obvious question is why anyone should pay for it.

The answer is that borrowed certainty compounds. Each time someone acts on your conviction as if it were fact, they take on a risk they didn't know they were accepting. And when it goes wrong, they don't just question the decision. They lose the ability to calibrate you. From then on, everything you say comes at a discount, including the things you actually know. The leader who labels uncertainty pays a small price up front. The one who hides it pays later, in full, usually in front of the people whose trust mattered most.

That isn't an argument for hedging everything. I don't want leaders talking like corporate attorneys, qualifying every sentence until nothing meaningful is left. Leadership means making judgments with incomplete information. Sometimes the right sentence is simply, *"This is what I believe we should do,"* even when the evidence isn't settled. Disagreement isn't irresponsibility, and strong convictions aren't a flaw.

The responsibility isn't to pretend uncertainty away or to drown in it. It's to name it, then decide anyway:

*Here's what we know. Here's what I believe it means. Here's what we don't know yet. And here's the decision I'm making.*

I trust that leader more, not less.

## The same problem, at machine scale

This distinction is becoming harder to maintain as AI becomes part of how organizations research, analyze, recommend, and decide. These tools can make the line unusually difficult to see because a verified figure, a reasonable inference, and a plausible hallucination can arrive in the same confident prose, formatted identically.

That doesn't make the tools the problem. It makes the leader's job clearer. When you put AI-generated analysis in front of your organization, you take responsibility for the claims you choose to carry forward under your name. Your audience may not know which sentence came from a verified source, which came from an inference, and which nobody has checked.

You should.

The standard doesn't transfer to the machine. It stays with whoever holds the microphone. Knowing what has been verified, what is interpretation, and what remains uncertain is now part of the leadership job. It is not a technical detail to delegate away.

## Words travel farther than the speaker

Leaders rarely control where their words finally land. An offhand comment in a leadership meeting becomes unofficial policy by lunch. A passing piece of career advice gets carried for a decade. A teacher's quick remark becomes part of how a student sees herself. A strong opinion shared with a team shapes how someone plans their next move. The person speaking may move on almost immediately, while the person listening sometimes doesn't.

A sentence can give someone permission to take a risk or talk them out of one. It can reinforce something harmful, challenge something deeply held, open a wound, or help close one. That's why a platform is more than an opportunity to be heard. It's a responsibility to the people who hear you.

## Three things we owe the people listening

I don't think this needs an elaborate leadership framework. For me, it comes down to three commitments.

**Label your convictions.** Saying "this is what I believe" doesn't diminish what you believe. It honors the person deciding whether to believe it with you. And if your argument gets weaker the moment you add that caveat, the problem isn't the caveat. It's the argument.

This isn't only a leadership lesson. My wife and I have been married long enough, and come through enough together, that people often ask me for relationship advice. I've learned that what works for us is not a golden ticket. It's our story, not a law of marriage. So I offer it that way, with room for grace, for empathy, and for the other person to disagree and go on their own journey. Being clear that a conviction is a conviction can be what saves a relationship.

**Show what you don't know yet.** There are things I believe strongly that I could still be wrong about. Both can be true at once, and holding them together may be one of the clearest markers of mature leadership. Certainty can make a person sound powerful, but honesty about its limits is what makes them trustworthy, and trust is the thing that lasts.

**Remember that someone may act on it.** Your audience isn't always grading your rhetoric. Often they're deciding what to do next. The more credibility you've built, the easier it becomes for someone else to borrow your certainty. That's worth remembering before you hand it out casually.

## What the higher standard actually means

People talk about leaders being held to a higher standard as though it were a penalty for visibility. I see it differently. The standard isn't higher because leaders are better than anyone else. It's higher because their words reach farther. A flawed assumption that affects only me is a private mistake. The same assumption delivered confidently to a team, a company, a classroom, or a following of thousands is something else entirely.

None of this means never being wrong. I've been wrong, and I will be again. The question is whether we're careful enough with our influence to keep the line visible, especially now, when anyone with a phone can build a platform and any tool can produce confident prose on demand.

So I come back to the question underneath that conversation with my friend: **what do we owe the people who trust us enough to listen?**

Not perfection. Honesty about what we know, what we believe, and what we don't know yet, and enough respect for the people listening to show them the difference.

The microphone is a privilege.

The weight comes with it.
