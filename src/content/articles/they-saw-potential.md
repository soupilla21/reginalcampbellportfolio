*Being called promising lands differently when you believe you've already delivered.*

An interviewer once told me they saw potential in me. I appreciated the sentiment. I also wondered what they thought I had been doing up to that point.

I had walked into that room believing I was a leader who had already delivered. I had led people, made difficult decisions, and lived with the results. There were things I would do differently and things I still needed to learn, but I did not think of my leadership as an ability waiting to emerge. It was work I was already doing.

Then came the word *potential*, and I found myself accepting a compliment that seemed to place me somewhere behind where I understood myself to be.

There is a familiar story about someone who sees something in you before you see it in yourself. A teacher notices an ability. A manager takes a chance. Someone lends you enough confidence to begin building your own. I understand why people treasure those moments. But I wasn't waiting to be discovered. That was what made the word difficult to receive.

## Promising, After Delivering

I wasn't looking for praise. I wanted the experience I brought into the conversation to count as experience, not merely as evidence that I might do something with it someday.

I had not been practicing on imaginary people or making decisions with hypothetical consequences. The responsibilities had been real. So had the mistakes. I had lived with what happened after a decision, when explaining my intentions was no longer enough and I still had to answer for the outcome. What part of that was waiting to become leadership?

For a moment, it felt like being moved backward while someone assured me I was going forward. I did not want credit for a future version of myself at the expense of the person who had actually shown up.

The interviewer may have meant they saw the possibility of leadership at a different scale. Being effective in one role does not make someone ready for every role. But the word alone did not tell me whether they recognized what I had done or were reserving their belief for what I might eventually do.

I could appreciate their confidence in my future and still feel that something in my past had gone unacknowledged.

## The Work of Explaining the Work

The interviewer had not worked alongside me. They had my background, our conversation, and whatever conclusions they could reasonably draw from them. I had years of experience with myself, and all the blind spots that come with that familiarity. My confidence was not proof they were obliged to accept.

But the interview required me to turn that experience into something another person could recognize. I had to select the right examples, supply enough context, and explain my contribution without claiming everyone else's. A decision that had taken days of difficult conversations might receive a few sentences. The circumstances that made an outcome meaningful could take longer to explain than the outcome itself.

That is part of interviewing. What unsettled me was how easily a failure of explanation could resemble a lack of ability. If I did not make something visible, would it be understood as something I had not yet developed?

"I haven't seen it" and "you haven't demonstrated it" can sound very close from the other side of a table. From my side, the distance between them felt considerable.

## Someone Who Could Explain Me

I have benefited from people who understood my work well enough to speak about it to someone else. They could give context to a decision that sounded ordinary in summary. They knew what had been difficult, what I had contributed, and what had changed. They did not have to reconstruct the experience from my account. They were there.

In that interview, I did not have that person.

I had to provide the example and explain why it mattered. I had to describe the difficulty without sounding as though I was making excuses, claim the contribution without exaggerating it, and convey confidence without allowing confidence to become the thing being evaluated. When an example did not connect, I had to decide whether to explain more or move on. More explanation might make the work clearer. It might also make me sound less certain of its value.

That is the exhausting part: having done the work, then having to establish the terms on which the work can be understood.

An advocate can relieve some of that burden. They can say, "I saw it," and give the listener a reason to stay curious. Without one, I was asking the interviewer to trust both my account of what happened and my judgment about what it meant.

I want to believe the work speaks for itself. I also know how much can disappear between doing something and describing it to a stranger.

That is the part I cannot shrug off. I could describe my experience accurately and still leave wondering whether the person across from me had met anyone I recognized.

## I Wanted Them to See It

I want people to see possibilities in me. I want responsibilities that stretch me and work that requires me to change. But I want the starting point to be visible. I want to discuss what comes next without surrendering my account of what came before.

And I cared whether that interviewer could see it. I was there because the opportunity mattered, and their judgment had consequences for it. Being confident in myself did not make me indifferent to their judgment.

That is harder to admit than irritation. I could be certain of what I brought and still want another person to recognize it. I could resent the word and still value the belief behind it. Neither feeling canceled the other.

I entered that room ready to talk about the leader I was and the work I could do next. The interviewer told me they saw potential.

Did they see more ahead of me, or less behind me?
