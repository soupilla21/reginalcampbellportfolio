*I spent more than a year out of work, helping strangers apply for jobs while nobody was calling me back.*

I didn't expect a Will Smith movie to make me cry.

*The Pursuit of Happyness* got me anyway. It tells the true story of Chris Gardner, a struggling San Francisco salesman who ends up homeless with his young son while competing for a single job at the end of an unpaid internship at Dean Witter. His story is not mine. I never had to lock myself and my child in a BART station bathroom for the night.

## That moment

Gardner gets the job. He walks out of that building, and there's a look on his face. Disbelief. Relief. Gratitude. No victory speech. He just walks.

That's when the tears came, and they came hard. Not because he got the job. Because I remembered what it was like not knowing whether I ever would.

## The year I wasn't sure I'd get across

I once left a good-paying, stable job for another company so I could do what I actually wanted to do: technology and project management. Not long after I joined, they laid me off. Then I was out of work for more than a year.

I've been shown the door more than once in my career. My mantra in life had always been simple: fool me once, shame on you. Fool me twice, shame on me.

This wasn't my first layoff. By my own application of that rule, I had run out of people to blame but myself. It landed square on my shoulders and challenged every ounce of my identity. I wasn't just questioning my career decisions anymore. I was questioning the man making them.

I still live by that rule. But I would judge that year differently now.

I have never handled setbacks gracefully. I don't pivot elegantly or land on my feet. I crash through, trip over myself, and drag myself across the line looking more beat up than triumphant. This was the time I wasn't sure I'd get across.

More than a year is long enough to start wondering whether the world has quietly decided you don't belong in it anymore. Long enough to send application after application, interview, wait, hope, get rejected, and watch your savings shrink while your confidence follows it down. Long enough to replay the decision to leave a stable job every single day.

Sade's "Feel No Pain" had been one of my favorite songs for years. It's a song about what unemployment does to a family. That year, it hit differently, usually in the car.

It nearly cost me everything. My wife and I had bought our first house, and our young one was growing up in it. That house was proof I had built something stable. My family depended on me to provide, and I was falling short in ways I could never have imagined. Every month without a paycheck brought us closer to losing it. And losing it would have felt like failing the people counting on me.

During that stretch, I volunteered with an organization that helped people learn to navigate the internet and use it to apply for jobs. I sat beside people and showed them how to get through the same process that kept turning me away.

Part of it was just staying busy. Getting out of the house beat sitting in it. But it was also one of the few places that year where I still felt useful. And none of the people I helped knew I was out of work too.

Both things were true in the same week. I couldn't get a hiring manager to call me back, and I could still help a stranger get an application in front of one.

That's where the call came. I stepped out of the room to answer it.

It was someone from HR. They asked if I was still interested. I hadn't been their first choice for the role I'd interviewed for, they told me, but another position had opened, and they wanted me for it.

Not the first choice. Still chosen.

I could barely get words out. Part of me didn't believe what I had just heard.

After I hung up, I was walking down the street crying, on the phone with my wife. We had a way out. We weren't going to lose the house, and at that point in our lives, that house was everything. The crying went on for hours.

Sade's song keeps coming back to a grown man coming apart. That day, I was one, on a city street. Mine just happened to be tears of relief.

So when Gardner walks out of that building without a word, I recognize the walk.

## What the ending leaves out

The film never lets Gardner know he's going to get the job. Every scene before the last one is played without that knowledge. That's what makes it honest.

The trouble with an ending like that is what we do with it afterward. We watch a man get the job and start treating everything he endured as the necessary price of getting there.

I can't tell my year that way. I got the call. Somebody else got the job I'd originally interviewed for. Plenty of people got the same rejections I did, without ever getting another call. I'd like to tell you the difference was perseverance, or preparation, or some quality that looks good on a poster. I don't know that. I know I kept going. I also know people keep going just as hard and the call never comes.

Getting the job changed my circumstances. It didn't make the year before it fair, and it didn't turn the hours I spent at that volunteer table into part of a plan. They were what I did while I didn't know.

I still wouldn't call myself a Will Smith fan. But I owe that one performance something. For two hours, it showed me the part of my own story where I didn't know how it would end.
